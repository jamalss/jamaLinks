Part One

The Root

Before meekness can be seen in a life, it has to be understood as a word. This is where it starts — buried in Hebrew soil, centuries before the Sermon on the Mount.

Meekness is the quality of being patient in the reception of injuries; it stands opposed to malice and revenge.

Strong's
H6035 · עָנָו
Transliteration

Phonetic

Origin
From H6031
TWOT entry
1652a
Part of speech
Noun, masculine
Strong's definition
Depressed, figuratively in mind (gentle) or circumstances (needy, especially saintly): humble, lowly, meek, poor. Compare H6041.
Oxford
Meek: mild.

The root behind the word means "to bend low" — but that bending happens two ways. Sometimes life forces someone low: poverty, oppression, affliction with no power to resist it. Sometimes a person bends low on purpose, in the presence of God. Scripture treats these as close cousins; both end up in the same posture, looking up.

This is not weakness. The same root can mean "to afflict" — ʿānāv describes someone who has the standing to assert themselves and chooses to bend anyway. Moses, "very meek, above all men" (Numbers 12:3), was not a doormat; he led a nation and stood before Pharaoh. The meek inherit the land (Psalm 37:11) not by retreating from it, but by refusing to seize it by force.

The word really has two sides of one coin. One side is circumstance — poor, oppressed, with nothing left to lean on but God. The other is character — a settled gentleness toward God and everyone else. Hebrew scribes mixed ʿānāv up with a close relative, ʿānî, so often that the two blur in the text. One leans toward the affliction; the other toward the humility it produces. Scripture blesses both — the one bent low by life, and the one who bends low on purpose.

Proverbs draws the contrast sharply: God opposes the proud but gives grace to the lowly (Proverbs 3:34). The proud stay rigid, holding their ground on their own strength. The meek stay flexible — because they've already put their weight on someone else.

Position
Bowed low — by life, or by choice.
Disposition
Gentle. Not self-assertive.
Dependence
Reliant on God, not on self.
Part Two

The Broken Warhorse

Scripture doesn't leave meekness as an abstract word. It hands you a picture: an animal built for war, answering a whisper.

Hast thou given the horse strength? Hast thou clothed his neck with thunder?... He mocketh at fear, and is not affrighted; neither turneth he back from the sword.
Job 39:19-25 KJV

In the ancient world, a warhorse was terrifying — trained to charge into noise and smoke without flinching. But before it was of any use, it had to be broken. Not broken down. Broken in. The wild will is tamed; the power stays completely intact.

  • 1
    It keeps all its strength. A broken horse can still crush a man or outrun a predator. Meekness isn't losing your power — it's choosing when to use it.
  • 2
    It answers the lightest touch. A wild horse ignores the rein. A broken one turns at a whisper of pressure.
  • 3
    It trusts the rider completely. It can't see the whole battlefield, and charges anyway, because it knows whose hand is on the reins.
  • 4
    It was subdued through love, not force. A good horseman doesn't break a horse by brutality — he earns its trust through patience.
  • 5
    Its power now serves the rider, not itself. The same strength that was once chaotic now carries someone else into what matters.

Jesus had the power to call down twelve legions of angels. He chose the cross instead.

Matthew 26:53 KJV
"I am strong enough to fight, but I choose Your rein."

A horse that stops fighting the rein hasn't lost anything. The same legs, the same power, all of it still there — just no longer spent resisting. Meekness is that same trade: not surrender, but strength finally pointed somewhere worth going.
